Social Enterprise Web

A technical strategy and infrastructure build for a startup social enterprise based in VIC. Navigating a $0 budget and severe server limitations, I had to find a way to bridge the gap between a founder’s big vision, an existing brand identity and the cold reality of unmanaged hosting for a scalable online presence.
  • Role: Web Strategist & Admin
  • Problem: Building a high-feature, low-cost web presence for a startup within severe technical and budgetary constraints.
  • Technical Strategy
  • Brand Integration
  • Self-hosted WordPress Via DigitalOcean

The Goal

To find a "Goldilocks" solution: something powerful enough to handle all the client's desired features for the online presence, but cheap enough to run on a startup budget with zero monthly SaaS fees.

The Users

I. The Founder (non-technical administrator)

II. The Social Enterprise Community (end-customers)

III. Potential Donors

The Core Problem

A $0 budget meant choosing "unmanaged" cloud server. We had the power, cost-saving and design flexibility of the self-hosted WordPress, but the server limitations created a performance ceiling that threatened the site's usability from day one.

The Challenge: The Cost of "Free"

Social Enterprise "PT" needed a professional online presence but was operating under extreme financial constraints. I spent almost a month researching, comparing, and then eventually presenting my finding to the founder over a Zoom call. To avoid the high monthly fees of Shopify or Squarespace, we went the self-hosted route via Digital Ocean.

The trade-off was immediate: "cheap" hosting meant limited server power. The backend became so sluggish that saving a single post was a chore. My challenge was determining if we were actually saving money, or if we were just burying the founder in technical debt they couldn't manage long-term.

Discovery & The Pivot

Because I couldn't just throw money at the problem, I had to be a researcher first. I mapped out a Comparative Analysis of CMS platforms against the client's specific needs.

After the initial build, I ran usability tests on the backend and realized the infrastructure was the bottleneck. It wasn't just a coding issue; it was an operational one. If the founder couldn't update their own site without a developer, the "free" solution was actually a failure.

Constraints: Brand vs. Bandwidth

The client already had an established Instagram presence and with a logo, color palette, and specific font types. Matching this identity was a major friction point.

Without the time or budget to hard-code a custom theme, I had to find the middle ground: implementing the brand kit within a resource-heavy environment (WordPress) without crashing the server. I had to make pragmatic calls at times. For instance, sacrificing a custom font weight to ensure the site actually loaded for the end customer.

The Solution: Strategy Over Software

Since the site's performance was at its limit, the real "product" I delivered wasn't just a URL. It was clarity.

1. Administrative Documentation
I created a comprehensive "How-To" guide that translated complex server management into simple steps. This is to ensure the founder had the technical power and ability to manage their own digital ecosystem without being dependent on a developer.

2. The Feasibility Report
I provided a data-backed (GPU chart) explanation of why their current infrastructure was a temporary fix and what tier of service they would need to reach their actual business goals.

Impact & Results

Success, to me, in this project meant giving the client the research and knowledge needed to make their own informed decision. While the site was eventually moved to a pro-bono provider, the work I did stripped away the "web jargon" and gave the founder a documented understanding of their digital plumbing and bottleneck for the first time.

What I Have Learnt

This project taught me that UX doesn't happen in a vacuum. Having the opportunity of seeing it from both a developer and designer mindset, UX is heavily influenced by technical and budget realities. I learned how to balance the "Designer's Vision" with the "Server's Capability."

It reinforced a core belief of mine: Designing a solution the client can't afford to maintain is just as bad as designing one they can't understand.

I learned to advocate for a path that is sustainable, even if it means making the tough call to pivot away from a "perfect" visual design for the sake of functionality.

This was quite a stressful project for me but a valuable learning experience.
