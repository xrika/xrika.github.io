Internal Service Design

A self-initiated service design project built from the inside. Working across E-commerce Operations and Customer Experience for over three years, I lived inside the broken system long enough to know exactly where it was failing and designed a low-code solution that fixed it with zero budget and zero new software installs.
  • Role: Systems Designer & Operations Liaison
  • Problem: Fragmented communication between remote CX and on-site fulfillment led to "ghost" reservations, lost inventory, and customer churn.

The Goal

To create a centralised "Source of Truth" that bridges the physical stockroom with the digital request, eliminating the single point of failure and making the system usable by anyone, at any time.

The Users

I. CX Agents (remote and on-site)

II. Liaison Officer (Operations)

III. Stock Control & Order Fulfilment Teams (on-site)

IV. The End Customer

The Core Problem

Google Chat was a leaky bucket. Messages were lost, tribal knowledge lived with one person, and when that person wasn't there so then the whole system would collapse.

The Challenge: The Invisible Customer

In a non-official click-and-collect environment, A.K.A "Reservation" stage, the "user" isn't just the person buying the shoes. It's the CX Agent taking the call, the Liaison Officer relaying the request, the Stock Controller locating the item, and the Order Fulfilment team setting it aside. Every one of them is a user and every handoff between them was a potential failure point.

I spent over three years working across every one of these roles; covering CX, stock processing, operations coordination, and at times acting as a 2IC. Because I sat across every team, I became the unofficial bridge between departments.

The problem was, that made me a single point of failure. When I wasn't there, the system would collapse.

Research & Discovery

My research was informal but evolving and core to my solution finding. After three years inside the operation, the pain points weren't hard to find as I was living them every day.

I had ongoing conversations with staff across every layer of the process: CX team leaders and agents, the stock and operations teams, the order fulfilment team, and retail staff in adjacent departments. I also observed friction firsthand while covering CX shifts, hearing customer frustration directly at the "moment of truth;" when they arrived to try on their reserved item and it couldn't be found or they had to relay the request all over again.

What I heard was consistent across every team:

— The CX team felt blind with no way of knowing whether their requests had been seen or acted on.

— The Stock Control team was frustrated by "Ghost" reservations where items physically moved with no digital trace.

— The Customers were forced to re-explain their entire request on arrival because nothing had been documented from the original call.

Nobody liked the system. But nobody had the time or drive to change it. So I did it myself, in my own time, on top of a full workload.

Mapping the Friction

I mapped the workflow state across four phases: Customer to CX, CX to Liaison Officer, Liaison Officer to Stock and Fulfilment, and Fulfilment to Customer.

The breakdown happened almost entirely in Phases 2 and 3 where all communication was verbal or via Google Chat with no confirmation loop, no documentation standard, and no physical-to-digital bridge.

The most damaging pattern was what I identified as the Single Point of Failure: only one person understood the full picture at any given time. If that person was unavailable then the chain broke.

Constraints

$0 budget. Zero new software installs permitted.

The solution had to be usable by team members across a wide range of technical confidence (from Excel-fluent to barely comfortable with a spreadsheet) and without costing anything to the business.

This is why I chose Excel over any SaaS tool. I wanted to design for the environment.

The Solution: A Low-Code Walk-In Excel Form and Process

I spent approximately six weeks researching, building, and refining an automated Excel-based workspace; all while teaching myself the formula logic required as I went. The system was built around three distinct layers, each serving a different user need.

1. The Logic Layer

A dedicated "How-To" documentation tab so that any team member could step in as liaison without needing to ask me how the system worked. This was the direct fix for the Single Point of Failure.

2. The Input Layer

A simplified data entry form for CX agents and Liaison Officer of all technical levels. Staff enter the customer's name, item details, and request. Clean, minimal, and no room for ambiguity.

3. The Output Layer (Physical Bridge)

An automated, locked print template that generates a standardised Reservation Paperwork. The Paperwork is then printed and split in two; one half goes with the physical item in the designated stockroom area, the other sits at reception.

When the customer arrives, the fulfilment team checks the file at reception and retrieves the item without hunting, without asking, without a single verbal relay.

4. The Zendesk Record

All requests and stock movements were simultaneously recorded on relevant Zendesk ticket(s) and in the physical walk-in tab, creating a dual digital and physical paper trail for the first time.

Impact & Results

The stock and operations team had previously spent anywhere from hours to multiple days tracking down a reserved item. From searching verbally, checking Google Chat history, asking around. After implementation, the item location was findable in seconds via the reception copy of the tag.

Beyond efficiency, the system created genuine reliability. Any team member could now act as liaison using the documentation provided, removing the dependency on any single person.

Customer complaints about items not being ready on arrival dropped significantly, and the handoff at the "moment of truth" became smooth and consistent for the first time.

What I Have Learnt

The biggest friction in this project wasn't technical. It was cultural. Everyone had quietly accepted the broken system as normal. There was no urgency to fix it because the pain was distributed across teams and nobody owned it entirely.

With my current UX knowledge, I'd have approached this very differently from the start. I'd map everything visually in FigJam or Miro to make the chaos visible and impossible to ignore, and present the "before" state to leadership as a documented problem before proposing any solution.

A clear visual of where the system was failing would have been far more persuasive than a verbal explanation, and would have given the project the formal support it deserved rather than being squeezed in around my actual workload.

What this experience gave me was really valuable and I feel it's something no bootcamp teaches. I know what it feels like to be every user in a broken workflow. The agent, the coordinator, the stock team, the customer at the door. That perspective is now how I approach every design problem.
