Go Casual

Go Casual is a mobile app that connects casual workers and employers in hospitality through a smart, streamlined platform. Workers can manage profiles, availability, and job preferences, while employers can pre-set roles, filter candidates, and manage compliance.
  • Role: UI Designer (6-month Contract in 2023)
  • Problem: Translating the client’s vision into a functional, scalable interface with a strict high-fidelity MVP scope while bridging the gap between physical branding and digital experience.

The Goal

To create a cohesive design system that allowed for a quick market launch (MVP) while maintaining a professional, high-end feel.

The User

I. Hospitality employers

II. Hospitality casual employees

The Physical Bridge

The client needed a way to drive traffic from physical business cards to a digital contact point seamlessly.

Brief Analysis

The project came to me through a Project Manager who served as the sole point of contact with the client so I never had direct access to the client.

Working from a relayed brief, I independently researched inspiration references and competitor apps in the gig economy and hospitality space to fill in the gaps and build a visual direction. I looked for patterns in tone, layout density, and colour language to reverse-engineer what a high-end but accessible hospitality platform could look and feel like, then brought those findings into my early design decisions.

User Stories

Along with the developers, we broke down the client's vision of the app on Google Doc. We dissected each idea and asked the questions to make sure the each idea is important and deliverable in both design and development stage.

Below is the visualisation of the final User Stories.

Decision Logic

The UI needed to work for two very different users at once; a time-poor hospitality manager posting a shift on the fly, and a casual worker checking their phone between services. That dual context drove every decision toward clarity.

I stripped back visual noise to reduce cognitive load for users who may be new to gig-economy apps, kept component patterns consistent to speed up developer handoff within the MVP scope, and maintained a restrained, professional aesthetic that reflected the client's high-end hospitality positioning.

MVP Main Functional Requirements

1. Dual-user onboarding

Separates role-specific profile setup flows for employers and casual workers, including credential verification and rate preferences.

2. Smart shift matching

Employers set job parameters (rate, credentials, location) and only receive responses from workers who meet them; workers only see shifts that match their availability and minimum rate.

3. Availability and calendar management

Workers can block out unavailable times, and the system automatically removes conflicting shift offers once a shift is accepted.

The Solution
Key Features

1. Calendar & Availability UI

One of the most logic-heavy screens to design. The calendar had to do two things at once: reflect the worker's manually set availability and automatically update when a shift was accepted, so they'd never receive a conflicting offer.

The design challenge was making that dynamic state feel intuitive at a glance, without overwhelming a user who might be checking the app in thirty seconds between shifts.

2. Onboarding Flow

Because the app serves two distinct user types with completely different needs, the onboarding had to branch early and cleanly.

I designed role-specific flows, employer and employee, that collected only what was necessary at sign-up, reducing drop-off risk. This was also where credential inputs (like RSA verification for bar work) had to feel trustworthy without feeling bureaucratic.

3. Shift Openings — Employer Side

The employer-facing shift creation screen was designed around the insight that managers would often be posting in a rush. I designed it around pre-set job templates so returning employers could fill a role in seconds, not minutes.

Filtering logic; by rate, credential, and prior performance was, surfaced clearly so employers felt in control without being overwhelmed by options.

4. Business Card & QR Ecosystem

I designed a minimalist business card featuring a QR code as a physical-to-digital entry point, a natural fit given the hospitality industry's shift toward QR-based menus at the time.

The card was designed to bridge a networking moment directly into a digital touchpoint, reducing friction for investors and businesses the client was pitching to.

What I have learnt

This was my first serious dive into UI/UX and my first time working in Figma (!)

I came in with an Adobe Creative Suite background and learned the tool and processes entirely on the job. That alone was a steep and worthwhile curve.

But the most valuable lesson wasn't about software. It was about process. Without a project tracker like Jira, and with communication flowing through an overloaded PM, misaligned expectations were almost inevitable.

Early on, I built out far more screens than the MVP scope required. The PM had seen the work and acknowledged it, only to later flag it wasn't MVP-compliant. That meant rebuilding from scratch, which cost us both time.

A similar issue surfaced with the business card: a detail that could have been caught in a quick review round led to reprints, and real costs.

What I took away is that a good design process protects everyone. Present me now insist on a shared working space, e.g Miro or FigJam over a flat Google Doc so that feedback is visual, traceable, and harder to misread.

I'd also push for scope to be locked in writing before a single frame is designed. The instinct to dive in and build is still there; the discipline to align first is what I've developed and try to keep improving since.
